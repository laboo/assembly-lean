# The Building in Lean: the cell's arithmetic, running and checked

A proof that runs, written in Lean 4 (version 4.35.0). The machines use only whole numbers; Mathlib, the standard library of checked mathematics, serves as the referee.

**Where the files live:** the GitHub repository [laboo/assembly-lean](https://github.com/laboo/assembly-lean), together with the compiled library. The Claude project keeps only this README.

**Which way is up** (the same as in the model): up means finer, down means coarser. The **ground floor** holds a spot's ones digit. A whole-number spot's address uses the ground floor and the floors beneath it (finitely many); the finer floors above the ground floor go on forever. Moving something down a floor turns it three-eighths of a lap and grows it; moving it up a floor shrinks it back. The climbing machines start just above the ground floor and work up, one finer floor at a time.

**Piece 1: the ground floor's addresses.** What Lean has checked (in `Building.lean`, 42 theorems, no gaps):

1. Every spot on the grid has an address made of 0s and 1s, one digit per floor, using the twist −1 + i.
2. No two different addresses name the same spot.
3. Adding two addresses digit by digit, starting at the ground floor and passing a carry down one floor at a time, gives exactly the address of the sum, and the carry is always one of 15 values, so the adder is a finite, local machine.
4. Multiplying two addresses using nothing but that adder gives exactly the address of the product: the complex plane's turning rule comes out of 0s and 1s.

**Linked to Mathlib's complex plane** (in `BuildingComplex.lean`, checked against Mathlib v4.35.0, the standard library of checked mathematics): reading an address in Mathlib's own complex numbers is Penney's formula, digit + (−1 + i)·(digit + …); every point with whole-number coordinates has an address; no two addresses land on the same point; and the 0-and-1 adder and multiplier land exactly on Mathlib's sum and product. These statements use only Mathlib's `ℂ`, `I`, `+` and `·`.

**Piece 2: the finer floors** (in `StreamMachine.lean`, plain Lean, and `BuildingStream.lean`, checked against Mathlib): an endless address hands over one mirror digit (0, 1, −1, i, −i) per finer floor above the ground floor. Lean checks that reading an endless address in Mathlib's plane always settles on one point; that every point of the unit disk has an endless address; and that a machine adding two endless addresses by climbing, one finer floor at a time and never looking further up, lands exactly on Mathlib's sum (the sum comes out shifted: moved six floors down, which multiplies it by 8i, what it writes is the true sum). The machine only ever holds one of 85 leftovers, and each digit it writes depends only on the floors read so far.

**Piece 3: multiplying endless addresses** (in `MulMachine.lean`, plain Lean, and `BuildingMul.lean`, checked against Mathlib): a machine multiplies two endless addresses by climbing, writing one floor of the product per floor read and never looking further up. What decides each digit is a leftover that Lean proves always stays inside a four-pointed star (4·|east| − |north| ≤ 2 and 4·|north| − |east| ≤ 2), because the five mirror digits cover the star, turned and grown by one floor, with room to spare. The product lands exactly on Mathlib's: −64 times the written product is x · y (moving it twelve floors down multiplies it by −64). Unlike the adder, the multiplier must remember everything read so far, the way long multiplication on paper does.

**Piece 4: the translator, over the whole plane** (in `FullMachine.lean`, plain Lean, and `BuildingFull.lean`, checked against Mathlib): a full address holds one mirror digit on every floor, finitely many whole-number floors (the ground floor and beneath) and endlessly many finer floors above (one digit story); on the whole-number floors, arithmetic writes the tidy plain 0/1 spelling. Every point of Mathlib's complex plane has one, and adding and multiplying full addresses, using only the machines from pieces 1–3, land exactly on Mathlib's sum and product.

**Piece 5: the mirror flip** (in `FlipMachine.lean`, plain Lean, and `BuildingFlip.lean`, checked against Mathlib): the mirrored twist is the twist turned a quarter turn, so a full address is flipped floor by floor with no carrying. Lean checks that this lands on Mathlib's mirror image (conjugate), that a full address times its flip is its squared size, and that plain 0s and 1s flip into exactly the five mirror digits.

**Piece 6: the address finder** (in `FindMachine.lean`, plain Lean, and `BuildingFind.lean`, checked against Mathlib): hand the machine any point of the plane as approximations (a rule that, asked for precision m, gives a whole-number spot on a halving grid within 1/2^m of the point), and it writes the point's full address, climbing one floor at a time. Lean checks that the address names exactly that point: the finder's leftover stays inside the four-pointed star even though it only ever sees approximations, because the star's wiggle room absorbs their error.

**Piece 7: breaking apart** (in `BuildingBreak.lean`, checked against Mathlib): our spots are Mathlib's whole-number points of the plane (its Gaussian integers), with the same adding, turning and mirror flip. Lean checks that a prime leaving remainder 1 or 2 when divided by 4 breaks apart into a turned piece times its mirror image (5 = (2 + i)(2 − i); Fermat's two-square theorem), that a prime leaving remainder 3 never breaks apart at all, and that breaking apart is always the same up to quarter turns of the pieces (unique factorization, Gauss). This piece is known mathematics brought in from Mathlib, connected to the building.

**Piece 8: landing, floor by floor** (in `LandMachine.lean`, plain Lean, and `BuildingLand.lean`, checked against Mathlib): a coin handed in from outside picks 0 or 1 on each finer floor. Lean checks that the spot it lands on is just the choices it made (after n floors, different chains of flips land on different spots), and that each flip narrows where it lands by one floor's shrink, 1/√2: after n flips the landing point is within 5/2 × (1/√2)^n of the spot those flips chose. The odds of the flips stay outside the building, as in the model's open question. The program picks from the ground floor up, which matches the model since v1.2.0 ("landing undoes the climb").

**Piece 9: the step rule, a toy** (in `StepMachine.lean`, plain Lean, and `BuildingStep.lean`, checked against Mathlib): space is a row of cells (one direction only), and each cell holds two arrows, one heading right and one heading left. Two are forced: no total-keeping rule that looks only at neighbors works with one arrow per cell (Meyer, 1996). Each tick, every arrow carries on or turns around, turning costs a quarter turn, and then every arrow steps one cell its way: Feynman's checkerboard (1965), today called a quantum walk. The machine uses whole numbers only; the exact rule's shrink of 1/√2 per tick is the size of one floor's shrink, so each tick is read one floor finer: each tick climbs one floor. Lean checks that nothing moves faster than one cell per tick, that the total of the arrows' squared sizes doubles exactly each tick (read one floor finer, the odds always add up to one; in Mathlib's own terms, one tick keeps the total), and that undoing a tick gives back every arrow, doubled. Starting from one arrow of size 1, after t ticks there are exactly 2^t tickets (squared sizes), so exactly t fair coin flips pick where the particle lands: one coin flip per floor climbed. Every chain of 6 flips was checked by Lean (and every chain of 8 by the demo) to land in each cell exactly as often as the cell's tickets say. The odds rule (squared size) is used as a labeled placeholder, as in the model's open question.

**Piece 10: which masses the floors allow** (in `MassMachine.lean`, plain Lean): in the checkerboard, mass is how often an arrow turns around. If each tick climbs whole floors, the carry-on and turn parts' squared sizes must add up to a power of 2, and Lean checks that this leaves only three cases: the parts are the same size (the toy's one mass, enormous in grid units), the turn part is zero (no mass: light), or the carry-on part is zero (always turns: stays put). The reason is plain arithmetic: two sums of two squares that add up to a power of 2 are equal, or one is zero. So with whole floors per tick, light comes free and every ordinary mass needs more.

**Piece 11: lighter masses, by turning only every n-th tick** (in `SlowMachine.lean`, plain Lean): an arrow turns only on every n-th tick and simply carries on in between, so nothing new is added. Lean checks that this is exactly the toy running on a grid n cells coarser: the cells r, r + n, r + 2n, … run period after period exactly like the toy's row, and they never depend on the cells in between. Measured on the fine grid, the particle's steps are n cells and n ticks long while light still moves one cell per tick, so its mass is 1/n of the toy's, for any whole number n. The price is that a particle of mass 1/n only ever sees every n-th cell; standard physics has the same kind of limit (a particle can't be pinned down finer than its Compton wavelength, and the lighter it is, the coarser that limit). A reading, not a result: a particle's mass becomes a whole number, the count of ticks between turns. **Caution (found later, Oct 15 evening):** such a particle's rest energy and its inertia (resistance to being pushed) disagree by a fixed factor of π/4 for every n, as they already do for the toy, so it does not obey E = mc²; a real electron needs small turns, which whole floors on two arrows can't make (piece 10).

**Piece 12: two particles on a line** (in `PairMachine.lean`, plain Lean): under the cell reading, space is a row of places, and two particles get one pair-cell for every pair of places ("one cell for each way they could be arranged," as the model says), each holding four arrows, one per combination of directions. Each particle's tick is the toy's step on its own place and direction. Lean checks that each particle's tick exactly doubles the total (read one floor finer per tick, the odds add up to one); that anything done to particle 2 alone that keeps its total, including moving it or leaving it frozen, leaves particle 1's own odds exactly as they were, so a link can never carry a message; and that the linked pair ("1 heading right and 2 heading left, minus the reverse") can't be split into arrows for particle 1 times arrows for particle 2: linked particles are exactly what can't be factored apart (the model's matching table, v0.6). Checked by running: who moves first doesn't matter. The demo shows the linked pair tending to fly apart (if particle 1 lands at +2, particle 2 lands at −2 in 136 of 192 tickets) and the room growing as a product (9 places each need 81 pair-cells). Where the pair-cells live is the model's open question.

**Piece 13: three directions of space** (in `Space3Machine.lean`, plain Lean): a cell at every place in three directions, two arrows per cell, and a free particle with no mass. One tick is three moves, one per direction: before each move the two arrows are turned so that, for that direction, one points forward and one back; then each steps one cell its way. This is operator splitting, the standard step-by-step construction of the Weyl and Dirac equations; Arrighi, Nesme and Forets (2014) proved it approaches the real equation as the grid gets finer. The turns are whole-number turns (adding and quarter-turning the two arrows), each doubling every cell's squared size exactly: one floor per turn, three per tick. Lean checks that each turn doubles every cell exactly and can be undone, that each move only carries arrows to a neighbor and can be undone exactly, that the three turns together come back to the start (three floors down), and that what a place holds after a tick depends only on the 27 places around it. Measured by running, not proved: at long wavelengths the particle moves at the same speed in every direction (along the long diagonal it lags by 0.4% at the longest wavelength tried, shrinking in step with the wavelength), so the grid's directions only show up at short wavelengths.

**Piece 14: the compiler, small turns from whole-number reflections** (in `CompileMachine.lean`, plain Lean): physics needs turns by arbitrary amounts (a mass, the pull of a charge), but the building's exact single steps are a small fixed set (piece 10; in quantum-computing terms, the Clifford turns). As quantum computers do, each wanted turn is compiled into exact whole-number steps. The step is a reflection across a direction made of four whole-number spots whose squared length n is a power of 2; Lean checks that it multiplies every four-arrow cell's squared size by exactly n² (it climbs floors and creates or loses nothing). Two reflections across nearly parallel directions make a small turn; the directions can't lie exactly in the wanted plane (piece 10), so each turn carries a tiny tilt, and pairing it with its mirror image cancels the tilt. One compiled mass turn is written out in exact whole numbers (four directions of squared length 2^40; 320 floors), and Lean checks that it keeps every cell's total exactly. Measured by running, not proved: with this turn the electron's rest energy and inertia agree as closely as with an exact turn on the same grid (E = mc², up to the grid's own effect, which vanishes as the grid gets finer), and its spin splitting is about 2 parts in 100 million of its rest energy; without the mirror pairing it is about 2 parts in 1,000.

**The compiler program** (`tools/compile.py`, Python; needs `mpmath` and `sympy`): given any turn size and accuracy, it finds the whole-number directions automatically (exact integers throughout), pairs them with their mirror images, measures the error with 60-digit arithmetic, and reports the floors the turn costs. `python3 tools/compile.py` prints a table; `python3 tools/compile.py --lean 0.001 1e-7 > T.lean` writes the turn as a Lean file, and `lake env lean T.lean` has Lean check that it keeps every cell's total exactly (a few seconds). Measured: a turn of 0.1 needs 384 floors for 1 part in 1,000 accuracy, 512 for 1 in 100,000 and 704 for 1 in 10 million; a turn of 0.001 needs 512, 704 and 896. So each hundredfold gain in accuracy costs roughly 150 to 200 more floors, and smaller turns cost a little more.

**To run it yourself:** install Lean (https://lean-lang.org), then in this folder run `lake exe cache get` (downloads Mathlib pre-checked), `lake build BuildingComplex BuildingStream BuildingMul BuildingFull BuildingFlip BuildingFind BuildingBreak BuildingLand BuildingStep MassMachine SlowMachine PairMachine Space3Machine CompileMachine` (checks every proof, including the Mathlib links), and `lake build && .lake/build/bin/building` (runs the demo, about 2 seconds).

**In a Claude workspace** (where Mathlib's download is blocked): the files and the compiled library are in the GitHub repository `laboo/assembly-lean`; clone it and run `bash setup.sh` (about a minute), and everything checks in seconds.

**Piece 15: shadows, pure geometry** (`ShadowCert.lean`, `ShadowExamples.lean`, and the program `tools/shadow_workbench.py`; no physics). An object is a list of whole-number corners in any number of dimensions; a shadow map tilts it exactly and keeps three directions. The program builds objects (the 4D cube, the 16-cell, the 24-cell, the 5D cube, the simplest 4D solid), casts their 3D shadows, describes them (corners, edges, faces, symmetry, size), and answers "can this object cast this shadow?". Quick restrictions rule cases out (too many corners, too wide, lopsided where the object is symmetric, a lopsided face where every face of the object is symmetric); otherwise a search matches corners to corners and returns an exact tilt, with a certificate. `ShadowCert.lean` holds one checker, written once, that reads a certificate with whole-number arithmetic only, and one theorem proved once for every certificate (`Shadow.Cert.sound`): a passing certificate means the tilt keeps lengths and right angles, and the outline of the object's shadow (Mathlib's convex hull over ℚ) is the outline of the listed corners, whose corners (Mathlib's extreme points) are exactly the listed ones. `ShadowExamples.lean` holds 11 certificates written by the program: the 4D cube straight on (a plain cube) and corner first (14 corners, 12 four-sided faces), and nine shadows of the 4D cube, 16-cell and 24-cell at hidden random tilts that the search recovered. Lean's kernel checks all 11 in about 4 seconds. Run `python3 tools/shadow_workbench.py` from this folder to regenerate them. Not yet in Lean: certified "no" answers; the program's restrictions are standard facts of geometry, not yet proved here.

**Not yet covered:** the electron in three directions run end to end (piece 13's motion plus piece 14's compiled mass turn), a proof (rather than a measurement) that compiled turns get as close as wanted, particles that act on each other (pieces 9 to 12 have no forces), and where the pair-cells live. The wording pass is done: comments, the README and the demo's labels now say "up" for finer, as the model does. Two code names were left as they are so that no checked statement changes: the mirror digits `up` and `down` mean a step north or south on a floor (i and −i), and `lift` moves an endless address down, lifting digits out of its endless part.

**Piece 16: the strict core, model v4.0.0 as a locked program** (Oct 16, 2026, evening; `StrictCore.lean`, `StrictCore/`, `core/`). The author asked for the model "strictly as a program that can't be f'ed with": things can be put in and measured, but the model itself can't be changed. Read `core/RULEBOOK.md` first: every rule is tied to a sentence of the model, and wherever the model is silent the core stops with "the model doesn't say."
- `StrictCore/Compass.lean`: the compass on any floor in whole numbers (every point carries everything its floor's directions make when multiplied together; each piece is a set of directions, multiplied by counting swaps). Things use everything their floor's turnings make (rule 1); a turn is multiplying by a piece, a thing from one side and an operation from both.
- `StrictCore/Facts.lean` (checked by Lean's kernel): a thing has 1, 1, 2, 4 and 8 pieces on the point, line, plane, ours and the fourth floor; the plane's turning squared is minus one (it is the complex plane's i); a thing is minus itself after one full turn and back after two, while an operation is back after one; the fourth floor's volume piece squares to plus one, commutes with all six turnings and splits a thing into two halves of four (one for each handedness); our floor's volume piece behaves like i but is not a piece a thing may use.
- `StrictCore/Measure.lean`, `MeasureFacts.lean`: a magnet is the turning across its axis; a thing's answers are sorted against one reference turning fixed on its free side, and four different choices give identical odds on 81 prepare-then-measure pairs (so it is a convention, checked rather than proved in general); the odds come out as (1 + cos)/2. Axes must have whole-number lengths. Exploratory only (operations are a hard stop): polarizers on a plane-floor direction give the square of the cosine, and a magnet on a direction of our floor gives two answers.
- `StrictCore/Run.lean`, built as the program `strictcore`: reads one trial and answers odds, a named hard stop, or a refusal (unknown fields, a typed-in state on our floor, a spare piece given a job, an axis without a whole length). Run on its own, it stamps its answer "not logged: does not count."
- `core/referee.py` (no physics): `run NAME` seals the prediction in `core/log.jsonl` before opening `core/observed/NAME.json`, then compares with exact fractions; `poke NAME` or `poke --json '{...}'` logs exploration with its input; `verify` checks the log's chain; `report` lists every try and counts tries per kind of particle; `lock --by NAME` (only with the author's go) records the fingerprint in `core/LOCK.json`, and a changed core is refused until it is relocked.
